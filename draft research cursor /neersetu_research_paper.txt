ABSTRACT
========================================================================
Water-borne disease surveillance in resource-constrained regions is frequently reactive rather than preventive because environmental observations, symptom reports, and operational response systems are disconnected. This paper presents NeerSetu, an end-to-end early warning framework that unifies heterogeneous data streams, estimates short-horizon outbreak risk, and converts model outputs into operationally meaningful alerts. The central contribution is not only predictive modeling but a complete decision workflow that includes data quality governance, uncertainty-aware calibration, interpretation modules, threshold policy design, and response accountability.

The research investigates whether integrating water quality indicators, syndromic signals, weather context, and reporting reliability metadata improves near-term risk prediction over single-source baselines. Results from structured experiments on synthetic but policy-realistic datasets show consistent gains in recall and precision-recall tradeoff when multi-source features are used with calibrated gradient boosting models. The framework further introduces alert-tier mapping, confidence labeling, and audit trails to support accountable deployment.

This paper contributes: (1) a district-oriented data integration architecture, (2) a transparent machine learning pipeline with calibration and explainability, (3) an operational policy layer linking risk to action, and (4) a governance strategy for safe adoption. The findings indicate that practical surveillance intelligence requires co-design across epidemiology, software engineering, and public administration rather than model optimization alone.

Keywords: water-borne diseases, early warning system, public health informatics, explainable AI, risk calibration, surveillance analytics, decision support.

1. INTRODUCTION
========================================================================
Public health systems are strongest when intervention begins before escalation. For water-borne diseases, leading indicators often appear in environmental systems (water contamination spikes, sanitation disruptions, seasonal weather shifts) before clinical case loads peak. However, in many districts these indicators are not analyzed jointly, and data-driven prevention remains limited.

NeerSetu addresses this gap by building a predictive and interpretable surveillance layer that sits between field observations and administrative action. The platform is designed for realistic constraints: delayed uploads, variable data quality, mixed digital maturity, limited specialist staff, and high consequence of missed signals.

The research premise is simple: when multi-source weak signals are fused with robust quality controls and calibrated decision thresholds, early warning can improve without overwhelming administrators with false alarms.

1.1 Epidemiological and Operational Context
Water-borne disease risk is shaped by interacting pathways:
- contamination events and treatment failures,
- climate and hydrological dynamics,
- population behavior and mobility,
- health-seeking latency and reporting practices.

Traditional surveillance often emphasizes confirmed case counting. While essential, confirmed case data is inherently lagging. A predictive strategy should include preclinical and contextual proxies that offer early directional evidence.

1.2 Why Existing Systems Fall Short
Common systemic limitations include:
- Siloed ownership of water, health, and meteorological datasets.
- Inconsistent timestamping and schema standards.
- Inadequate representation of data uncertainty in dashboards.
- Binary alert rules that ignore confidence and context.
- Absence of action traceability (who acknowledged, who acted, when closed).

1.3 Research Rationale
A deployable solution must satisfy four constraints simultaneously:
1) predictive utility,
2) interpretability for non-ML stakeholders,
3) operational feasibility in low-resource settings,
4) governance compliance and accountability.

NeerSetu is built and evaluated under this four-constraint lens.

2. PROBLEM STATEMENT
========================================================================
District health and water authorities require actionable early signals of outbreak risk but currently depend on fragmented, delayed, and partially reliable inputs. The core problem is not raw data scarcity; it is the absence of a system that can convert noisy multi-source data into calibrated, explainable, and policy-aligned alerts.

Formally, let D_t represent all available observations up to day t across water quality, symptom trends, weather context, and reporting metadata. The objective is to estimate:

P(Y_{t+h}=1 | D_t), where h is the forecast horizon (3-7 days),

and convert this probability into an alert tier A_t in {Watch, Warning, Critical} such that:
- recall for high-risk events remains high,
- false alarm burden remains manageable,
- confidence and uncertainty are explicitly communicated.

5. RELATED WORK
========================================================================
5.1 Classical Surveillance
Statistical process control methods (CUSUM, EWMA) are transparent and easy to operationalize, but they assume relatively stable historical regimes and are sensitive to abrupt reporting artifacts.

5.2 Environmental Epidemiology
Many studies show association between rainfall extremes, water turbidity, and gastrointestinal disease incidence. However, associations often vary by geography, infrastructure quality, and lag structure.

5.3 Machine Learning for Forecasting
Recent work uses tree ensembles, temporal neural networks, and hybrid nowcasting systems. Strengths include nonlinear interaction capture; weaknesses include calibration gaps and deployment complexity.

5.4 Explainability in Public Health AI
Trust and accountability require interpretable outputs. Global feature importance is insufficient for field decisions; local case-level explanations and quality flags are needed.

5.5 Gap Synthesis
The key gap is translational: most studies optimize predictive metrics but under-specify workflow integration, governance ownership, and threshold maintenance. This paper addresses that translational gap.

6. NEERSETU FRAMEWORK OVERVIEW
========================================================================
NeerSetu is organized into six connected layers:

L1 Ingestion Layer
- Receives water, symptom, weather, and admin records via API.
- Validates schema and source signatures.

L2 Data Reliability Layer
- Computes completeness score.
- Tags delayed and low-confidence records.
- Harmonizes units and timestamp formats.

L3 Feature and Signal Layer
- Builds rolling, lagged, interaction, and anomaly features.
- Produces feature quality metadata.

L4 Prediction Layer
- Trained model returns risk probability and uncertainty indicators.

L5 Interpretation Layer
- Generates local explanation summary.
- Exposes top contributing risk drivers.

L6 Decision and Audit Layer
- Maps risk to alert tier based on threshold policy.
- Stores event logs for accountability and review.

7.1 Data Sources
S1 Water Quality Stream:
- pH, turbidity, residual chlorine, conductivity, coliform indicators.

- fever-like complaints, diarrhea complaints, clinic visit acceleration.

S3 Weather and Context Stream:
- rainfall, humidity, temperature variance, flood/overflow proxy.

7.2 Data Granularity
- Spatial: district and block aggregation.
- Temporal: daily aggregation with lag-aware alignment.
- Target horizon: 3-day and 7-day risk windows.

7.3 Labeling Strategy
Because this study uses synthetic but structurally realistic data, event labels are generated through domain-anchored rules combining contamination and health-signal escalation. Label generation is documented to preserve reproducibility and sensitivity analysis.

7.4 Data Dictionary (Condensed)
- `turbidity_ntu`: Nephelometric Turbidity Units.
- `residual_chlorine_mg_l`: disinfection residual indicator.
- `symptom_cluster_score`: normalized symptom burden proxy.
- `report_delay_days`: lag between event and upload.
- `target_event`: binary risk label within forecast horizon.

========================================================================
8. DATA QUALITY GOVERNANCE
========================================================================
8.1 Quality Checks
Q1 Schema compliance.
Q2 Range and unit validity.
Q3 Temporal consistency.
Q4 Duplicate and conflict detection.
Q5 Missingness characterization by source.

8.2 Completeness Score
A weighted completeness score C in [0,1] is computed per record:

C = sum(w_i * availability_i)

where w_i reflects variable criticality. C is later exposed in alert cards so users can interpret risk in quality context.

8.3 Missing Data Strategy
- Numeric environmental variables: median or seasonal median imputation.
- Temporal clinical trends: forward-fill with confidence penalty.
- Categorical reliability fields: explicit "unknown" category.
- Missingness indicators are retained as model features.

8.4 Delayed Reporting Handling
Records arriving late are aligned to event-day and flagged with delay metadata. Features include both corrected event values and a delay burden indicator.

========================================================================
9. FEATURE ENGINEERING
========================================================================
Feature design follows epidemiological plausibility and operational interpretability.

9.1 Temporal Features
- Rolling means (3, 7, 14 days).
- Exponential moving trends.
- Difference and acceleration metrics.

9.2 Lag Features
- Rainfall lag (1-7 days).
- Symptom lag progression.
- Chlorine depletion lag interactions.

9.3 Interaction Features
- `turbidity x symptom_rise`
- `low_chlorine x rainfall_spike`
- `high_delay x symptom_variance`

9.4 Stability Features
- Reporting volatility index.
- Block-level data consistency index.

9.5 Feature Selection
Procedure:
1) remove highly collinear candidates,
2) evaluate mutual information,
3) cross-check against domain rationale,
4) retain features with both predictive value and explanation clarity.

10. MODELING METHODOLOGY
========================================================================
10.1 Candidate Models
- Logistic Regression (baseline transparency).
- Random Forest (robust nonlinear baseline).
- Gradient Boosting (high tabular capacity).

10.2 Training Protocol
- Stratified split: train/validation/test.
- Time-aware split tested to reduce leakage risk.
- Class imbalance handled via weighted loss and threshold optimization.

10.3 Hyperparameter Tuning
Grid or randomized search over:
- tree depth,
- learning rate,
- estimator count,
- min samples per split/leaf.

10.4 Evaluation Metrics
Primary:
- PR-AUC (class imbalance sensitive),
- F1-score.

Secondary:
- ROC-AUC,
- recall at precision constraints,
- Brier score for calibration,
- expected calibration error (ECE).

10.5 Reproducibility Controls
- Fixed random seeds.
- Versioned artifacts for model, encoder, scaler.
- Logged parameter and dataset signatures.

========================================================================
11. CALIBRATION AND THRESHOLD POLICY
========================================================================
11.1 Why Calibration Matters
Uncalibrated probabilities may look confident but misrepresent true event likelihood. For policy operations, confidence honesty is as important as discrimination.

11.2 Calibration Techniques
- Platt scaling.
- Isotonic regression.

Calibration is selected using validation ECE and reliability curves.

11.3 Alert Tier Design
Tier 1 Watch:
- low-to-moderate rising risk,
- action: intensified observation.

Tier 2 Warning:
- moderate-to-high risk,
- action: targeted field testing, awareness advisories.

Tier 3 Critical:
- high risk with supporting signals,
- action: emergency coordination and rapid intervention.

11.4 Threshold Governance
Thresholds are not static forever. Review cadence:
- weekly operational sanity check,
- monthly governance board review,
- emergency override during atypical events (logged with reason).

Abstract
Water-borne disease management in resource-constrained regions is often delayed by fragmented reporting and weak integration between environmental and health data streams. This research paper presents NeerSetu, an interpretable multi-source early warning framework that combines water quality observations, symptom surveillance, weather context, and historical incidence trends to estimate short-horizon outbreak risk. The study contributes three outcomes: (1) a practical data model for district-level integration, (2) a calibrated machine learning pipeline for risk scoring, and (3) an operational alert policy that maps model output to response actions. Experimental evaluation on synthetic but policy-realistic datasets indicates that integrated modeling outperforms single-source baselines in recall at acceptable precision levels. The paper discusses deployment constraints, governance requirements, and ethical safeguards needed for real-world adoption.

Keywords
Water-borne disease surveillance, early warning systems, explainable machine learning, public health informatics, risk calibration, decision support.

1. Introduction
Outbreak control depends on lead time. In many public health workflows, by the time clinical case counts trigger concern, transmission and exposure may already be widespread. Water-borne illnesses such as cholera, typhoid, and acute gastroenteritis are especially influenced by changing environmental conditions, making them suitable for predictive monitoring that starts before clinical escalation.

NeerSetu is proposed as an integrated surveillance and early warning framework that reduces the delay between weak environmental signals and administrative action. Instead of replacing epidemiological practice, the framework augments it with continuous risk estimation and interpretable alerts.

2. Problem Context and Motivation
Most district-level systems face four structural limitations:
1) data silos between water authorities and health departments,
2) inconsistent reporting frequency across blocks,
3) delayed validation of field records, and
4) alert fatigue caused by noisy thresholds.

These limitations create a paradox: data exists, yet actionable insight arrives too late. The motivation for NeerSetu is to bridge this gap with a practical, low-friction intelligence layer that can operate on imperfect, asynchronous data.

3. Research Questions
RQ1. Can a unified feature space from environmental and clinical proxies improve short-term outbreak risk detection?
RQ2. Which model family provides the best balance between predictive quality and interpretability for district operations?
RQ3. How should probability outputs be calibrated and thresholded to support practical response tiers?
RQ4. What governance mechanisms are necessary to prevent misuse and maintain trust in machine-guided alerts?

4. Objectives
The research objectives are:
- Design a robust data integration schema for multi-source surveillance inputs.
- Build and evaluate a predictive pipeline for 3- to 7-day risk forecasting.
- Compare baseline and advanced models using policy-relevant metrics.
- Generate explanations that public health teams can understand and verify.
- Propose a deployable alert protocol tied to resource-constrained operations.

5. Related Work
Prior studies generally fall into three groups:
- Statistical outbreak surveillance (CUSUM, EWMA, seasonal decomposition),
- Environmental epidemiology models linking rainfall and contamination, and
- Recent machine learning systems for disease nowcasting.

Classical methods provide transparency but may miss nonlinear interactions. Deep models can capture complex signals but are often data-hungry and opaque. Recent literature increasingly supports hybrid approaches: feature-rich models with post-hoc explanation, calibration, and strong human oversight.

The gap remains in operational translation. Many papers optimize for benchmark performance but do not address district governance, alert ownership, and field constraints. This research emphasizes those practical dimensions.

6. System Overview
NeerSetu consists of five layers:
1) Ingestion Layer: water quality, symptom reports, weather observations.
2) Validation Layer: schema checks, missingness flags, timestamp normalization.
3) Feature Layer: rolling indicators, trend gradients, anomaly scores.
4) Modeling Layer: risk prediction with confidence calibration.
5) Decision Layer: alert generation, explanation cards, audit logging.

This layered approach supports modular updates. For example, model retraining can be improved without disrupting ingestion contracts.

7. Data Description
The study uses synthetic but structure-preserving datasets designed to mimic North-East India district operations. Variables include:
- Water: pH, turbidity, residual chlorine, coliform indicator.
- Health proxies: fever clusters, diarrhea complaint counts, clinic attendance shifts.
- Contextual: rainfall, temperature, humidity, local event intensity.
- Administrative: reporting delay, source reliability, block-level completeness.

Target variable:
- Binary risk event label for near-term outbreak probability window.

8. Data Quality and Preprocessing
Data quality determines model quality. The pipeline performs:
- Type normalization and unit harmonization.
- Outlier clipping using quantile thresholds and rule-based domain checks.
- Missing value imputation with source-specific strategy (median, forward-fill, indicator flag).
- Temporal alignment to daily index with late-report handling.
- Feature scaling for algorithms sensitive to magnitude differences.

A completeness score is computed per observation. Low-trust records are not discarded by default; instead, they are tagged so the model and dashboards can reflect uncertainty.

9. Feature Engineering Strategy
Predictive signals are engineered from domain logic:
- Rolling averages (3-day, 7-day, 14-day windows).
- Rate-of-change and acceleration features.
- Water-health interaction terms (for example, turbidity x fever-cluster rise).
- Weather-lag features to represent delayed contamination effects.
- Reliability-weighted symptom intensity.

Feature engineering prioritizes meaning over brute-force expansion. This reduces overfitting and improves explainability.

10. Modeling Approach
Candidate models evaluated:
- Logistic Regression (interpretable baseline),
- Random Forest (nonlinear, robust on mixed features),
- Gradient Boosting (higher capacity and often strong tabular performance).

Training setup:
- Stratified train-validation-test split.
- Class imbalance handling via weighted loss and threshold optimization.
- Hyperparameter search with cross-validation.

Primary metric: F1-score for imbalanced detection.
Operational metrics: Recall at fixed precision, PR-AUC, Brier score (calibration).

11. Calibration and Threshold Policy
Uncalibrated probabilities can mislead action. The study applies probability calibration (Platt and isotonic variants) and compares expected calibration error.

Alert thresholds are policy-driven:
- Tier 1 (Watch): rising risk, monitor more closely.
- Tier 2 (Warning): initiate targeted field sampling and advisories.
- Tier 3 (Critical): activate emergency coordination and rapid response.

Thresholds are tuned to preserve high recall for severe events while controlling alert volume.

12. Explainability and Human Interpretation
Model outputs are accompanied by explanation vectors:
- Top contributing features per alert.
- Directional impact summary (which factors increased/decreased risk).
- Confidence and data quality indicators.

This prevents "black-box directives." Officers can challenge and verify recommendations, improving accountability.

13. Experimental Results
Across repeated evaluation runs, integrated multi-source models consistently outperform single-source baselines.

Key observations:
- Logistic Regression remains useful for transparency but misses interaction effects.
- Random Forest improves robustness and recall.
- Gradient Boosting delivers the best overall discrimination and calibration after tuning.

Operationally relevant outcome:
The best-performing calibrated model increases early-detection lead time while keeping false alarms manageable under tiered thresholds.

14. Error Analysis
False positives are often linked to temporary symptom spikes without sustained water signal.
False negatives are associated with delayed reporting and abrupt cluster onset in low-coverage blocks.

Mitigation paths:
- Incorporate adaptive recency weighting for late reports.
- Include uncertainty penalties when completeness is low.
- Increase sampling density in historically under-reported blocks.

15. Deployment Architecture
Reference deployment uses:
- Backend API for ingestion and scoring,
- ML service for model lifecycle,
- Dashboard for analysts and district administrators,
- Mobile-ready alert channels for field officers.

All alert events are audit-logged with model version, feature summary, and decision metadata.

16. Governance and Policy Integration
Technical systems fail without institutional ownership. The deployment model defines:
- Alert ownership matrix (who acknowledges, who acts, who closes),
- SLA targets by alert tier,
- Escalation pathway for unresolved critical alerts,
- Monthly model review board with public health and technical stakeholders.

17. Ethical and Societal Considerations
Early warning systems can unintentionally amplify bias if reporting gaps correlate with marginalized geographies. Safeguards include:
- Transparency on data limitations,
- Fairness checks across districts/blocks,
- Human-in-the-loop confirmation before coercive interventions,
- Strict role-based access for sensitive records.

18. Threats to Validity
Internal validity risks:
- Synthetic data may not capture every real-world behavioral pattern.

External validity risks:
- Transferability to other states may require feature and policy adaptation.

Construct validity risks:
- Outbreak labels in synthetic generation may simplify nuanced epidemiological dynamics.

19. Comparative Discussion
Compared with static threshold systems, NeerSetu provides adaptive risk estimation and richer context.
Compared with high-complexity deep pipelines, the proposed approach is easier to deploy, explain, and govern in low-resource settings.

20. Practical Contributions
This research contributes:
- A deployment-ready blueprint for integrated surveillance,
- A calibrated and explainable predictive approach,
- A governance-centered framing that treats actionability as a first-class metric.

21. Limitations
Current limitations include:
- Dependence on periodic retraining for drift adaptation,

- Need for stronger geospatial micro-cluster modeling.

22. Future Work
Planned extensions:

23. Conclusion
NeerSetu demonstrates that practical early warning for water-borne diseases requires more than predictive accuracy. Real impact depends on integrated data design, calibrated risk interpretation, transparent explanations, and clear governance protocols. The proposed framework offers a realistic path from fragmented records to accountable preventive action.
